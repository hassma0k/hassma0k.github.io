Forecasts the Board Believes

Driver-based rolling forecasts

5 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Replace a wishful annual budget with a driver-based rolling forecast the board can believe and use.

⚡In 30 seconds

  • A budget is promised once; a forecast is re-earned every quarter.
  • Boards fund the CFO who is usually right, not always optimistic.
  • Hero numbers do not raise the forecast; they lower the board's trust in it.
  • Forecast with drivers; explain with sensitivities.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Explain how a rolling forecast differs from a fixed annual budget.
  • Rebuild profit from a few drivers and show which carries the risk.
  • Spot hero numbers and bias, and reset assumptions to a credible range.

💡The idea

Think of a ship's navigator. An annual budget is a course set in January and never checked. A rolling forecast updates the position every quarter and always looks four to six quarters ahead. Nobody trusts a navigator who reports 'exactly on course' daily; they trust one who is usually right and flags currents early.

Build the forecast from a few drivers, the things that really move the business: volume, price, cost inflation, capex timing. Change a driver and everything updates. Avoid hero numbers: growth above what the order book supports, inflation below signed contracts, capex spent faster than projects ever spend. Boards discount them, and trust in every number falls.

🧮The maths

Driver-based operating profit

Profit = Revenue₀ × (1 + g) − Costs₀ × (1 + i)

Revenue₀, Costs₀
last year's revenue and operating costs
g
revenue growth driver
i
cost inflation driver

Forecast bias

Bias = average of (Forecast − Actual) ÷ Actual

Forecast
what you predicted for a period
Actual
what actually happened
Bias
above zero: optimism; below zero: sandbagging

✍️Worked example: Resetting a hero draft

Last year: revenue SAR 1,000M, costs SAR 820M, profit SAR 180M. The draft assumes 18% growth, 2% inflation and 80% of SAR 200M capex in H1. Evidence supports 9% growth, 5% inflation and a 50/50 capex split.

The working, step by step
  1. Draft: 1,000 × 1.18 − 820 × 1.02 = 1,180 − 836.4 = 343.6.
  2. Credible: 1,000 × 1.09 − 820 × 1.05 = 1,090 − 861 = 229.
  3. Gap 343.6 − 229 = 114.6: growth 1,180 − 1,090 = 90, inflation 861 − 836.4 = 24.6.
  4. Sensitivities: each growth point = 1,000 × 1% = 10; each inflation point = 820 × 1% = 8.2.
  5. Capex: draft puts 200 × 80% = 160 in H1 versus 100 at 50/50, so 60 more cash out early.

Answer. Present SAR 229M with the two sensitivities, not SAR 343.6M. A number the board believes beats a higher one it discounts.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Using the stretch target as the forecast.

✓ Keep two numbers: the target that motivates and the forecast you would bet on.

✗ Forecasting hundreds of line items.

✓ Model the few drivers that explain most of the variance.

✗ Front-loading capex because that is when projects are approved.

✓ Phase capex on how your projects have actually spent in the past.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1Last year: revenue SAR 500M, costs SAR 400M. The draft uses 15% growth and 2% inflation; you reset to 8% and 5%. How much does forecast profit fall?

Draft 575 − 408 = 167; reset 540 − 420 = 120; fall 47. 35 and 12 count one change each; 23 nets them, but both cut profit.

2Which of these is a rolling forecast?

Rolling means the horizon moves with you. A fixed year-end horizon shrinks to nothing; one quarter is too short; a stretch target is not a forecast.

3For eight quarters running, your revenue forecast has come in 4–7% above actual. What should you conclude?

Random errors fall on both sides; eight misses one way is bias. A blanket 5% haircut hides the symptom and leaves the faulty drivers in place.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

A credible forecast gives up the pull of a stretch number and costs FP&A time every quarter. Keep targets and forecasts separate: a stretch target can motivate sales, but the board commits capital on your best estimate.

🧩 When the rule breaks

Drivers fail in start-ups, turnarounds or one-off shocks, where history says little; use base, downside and upside scenarios instead. Bands shift with the cycle. And sandbagging backfires: a forecast you always beat also gets discounted and starves good projects.

🏢 In real companies

Many large companies now run quarterly rolling forecasts beside a lighter annual plan for targets and pay. In the Gulf, oil prices drive government spending and project awards, big project capex often slips, and Ramadan moves about eleven days earlier each year, shifting demand between quarters. Fixed quarterly patterns get this wrong; drivers fix it.

💼 In an interview

“Why move from an annual budget to rolling forecasts?”

An annual budget is out of date within months. A rolling, driver-based forecast updates every quarter from the real drivers — volume, price, inflation, capex timing — so decisions use current information. The value comes from credible assumptions: realistic growth and cost inflation. A forecast the board trusts reduces surprises, lowers perceived risk and makes capital decisions faster.

📖 Key terms
Rolling forecast
A forecast updated each period that always looks the same distance ahead.
Hero number
An assumption chosen because it looks good, not because evidence supports it.
Forecast bias
Forecasts landing consistently above, or below, actual results.
Sandbagging
Deliberately forecasting low so results always beat the plan.
Capex phasing
How capital spending is spread across the periods of the plan.
