Forecasts the Board Believes

Driver-based rolling forecasts

3 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Should the board get a wishful annual budget or a driver-based rolling forecast it can trust?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • A budget is promised once; a forecast is re-earned every quarter.
  • Boards fund the CFO who is usually right, not always optimistic.
  • Hero numbers don't raise the forecast; they lower the board's trust.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Explain how a rolling forecast differs from a fixed budget.
  • Spot hero numbers and bias before the board does.
  • Judge which few drivers carry the forecast's risk.

💡The idea

A ship's navigator who sets a course in January and never checks it is an annual budget. A rolling forecast fixes the position every quarter and looks four to six quarters ahead. Boards trust navigators who flag currents early.

Build it from a few drivers: volume, price, cost inflation, capex timing. Avoid hero numbers, assumptions chosen to look good, not because evidence supports them. Boards discount them, and trust in every other number falls. Credibility is what gets capital committed.

🧭Decide like a CFO: Resetting a hero draft

FP&A's draft shows a big profit jump on 18% growth, cost inflation below contracted rises, and front-loaded capex. Evidence supports about 9% growth. The board meets next week.

Send the hero draft → looks great today; the board discounts it and every later number.

Sandbag it low → easy to beat, but starves good projects and is discounted too.

Reset drivers to the evidence, show sensitivities → a lower number the board will fund.

The call. Present the credible reset with its key sensitivities. A number the board believes beats a higher one it discounts.

🧮The formula, in plain words

Forecast bias

Bias = average of (Forecast − Actual) ÷ Actual

For revenue: above zero means habitual optimism, below zero sandbagging. Near zero earns trust.

🎮How it plays in the game

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Using the stretch target as the forecast.

✓ Keep two numbers: the target that motivates, the forecast you would bet on.

✗ Forecasting hundreds of line items.

✓ Model the few drivers that explain most of the variance.

✗ Front-loading capex because that is when projects are approved.

✓ Phase capex on how past projects actually spent.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1Sales wants the stretch target used as the board forecast 'to keep ambition high'. What do you do?

The board commits capital on the forecast. A stretch target can motivate, but blending or sandbagging still misleads the board.

2The board wants forward visibility beyond December. Which forecasting approach should you introduce?

Rolling means the horizon moves with you. A fixed year-end horizon shrinks to nothing; one quarter is too short.

3For eight quarters running, your revenue forecast has come in 4–7% above actual. What should you do?

Random errors fall both sides; eight misses one way is bias. A blanket haircut hides the symptom and keeps faulty drivers.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

A credible forecast gives up the pull of a stretch number and costs FP&A time each quarter. Keep targets and forecasts separate: a stretch target motivates sales; the board commits capital on your best estimate.

🧩 When the rule breaks

Drivers fail in start-ups, turnarounds and one-off shocks, where history says little; use base, downside and upside scenarios instead. Bands shift with the cycle. Sandbagging backfires too: a forecast you always beat gets discounted.

🏢 In real companies

Many large companies now run quarterly rolling forecasts beside a lighter annual plan for targets and pay. In the Gulf, oil prices drive public spending, big capex often slips, and Ramadan moves about eleven days earlier each year.

💼 In an interview

“Why move from an annual budget to rolling forecasts?”

An annual budget is out of date within months. A rolling, driver-based forecast updates every quarter from the real drivers — volume, price, inflation, capex timing — so decisions use current information. The value comes from credible assumptions: realistic growth and cost inflation. A forecast the board trusts reduces surprises, lowers perceived risk and makes capital decisions faster.

📖 Key terms
Rolling forecast
A forecast updated each period, always looking the same distance ahead.
Hero number
An assumption chosen because it looks good, not because evidence supports it.
Sandbagging
Deliberately forecasting low so results always beat the plan.
