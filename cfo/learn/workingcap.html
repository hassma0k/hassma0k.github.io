Cash Stuck in Stock

Cash-conversion cycle · supplier financing

3 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Which stock and supplier days to cut for cash, without starving sales or souring suppliers?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Stock is cash already spent and not yet earned back.
  • Cut slow movers, not safety stock.
  • Supplier credit is free only until someone prices it back.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Spot surplus stock that ties up cash without protecting sales.
  • Judge when an early-payment discount beats holding cash.
  • Weigh supply-chain finance, dynamic discounting and factoring.

💡The idea

Think of a family pantry. Enough food to reach the next shop, plus a little for surprises, is good planning. Six months of tins is not safety; it is money on a shelf, nearing its use-by date.

Protect safety stock on fast sellers; cut slow movers nobody is waiting for. Pay suppliers later only where it is free: skipping an early-payment discount is usually borrowing far above your WACC. Freed cash can repay debt or fund growth.

🧭Decide like a CFO: The overstocked distributor

A distributor holds 82 days of stock, mostly slow movers, against a 50-day need. It takes a supplier’s early-payment discount, and other suppliers are paid well within their tolerance. The board wants cash.

Cut slow movers, protect fast-seller buffers → the biggest cash release, with little sales risk.

Drop the early-payment discount → more supplier days, but forgoing it costs far more than WACC.

Stretch discount-free suppliers modestly → extra cash at little cost, if they do not price it back.

The call. Most of the cash sits in the warehouse. Cut slow stock, keep the discount, and stretch only free supplier days.

🧮The formula, in plain words

Annual cost of skipping an early-payment discount

Cost = d ÷ (1 − d) × 365 ÷ (N − D)

The discount you forgo as a yearly borrowing rate; d = discount, N = due day, D = discount day.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • Sliders open bloated: 61 days to collect, 82 of stock, 45 to pay. Leaving them is a D.
  • ‘Hold inventory’ is the big lever: each day removed frees cash and lifts EVA by cash × WACC.
  • Pushback starts below about 35 days of stock or 40 to collect, or beyond 60 to pay.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Cutting every product line by the same percentage

✓ Segment by turnover: cut slow movers hard, protect safety stock on fast sellers.

✗ Skipping discounts to flatter supplier days

✓ Annualise each discount; if it beats your borrowing rate, pay early.

✗ Counting only the capital charge on surplus stock

✓ Add storage, insurance, shrinkage and obsolescence; the true carrying cost is higher.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1A retailer holds far more stock than peers, mostly slow movers. What is the best first move?

Slow movers tie up cash nobody is waiting for. Blanket cuts hit fast sellers and lose sales; longer terms leave the excess.

2A supplier offers a small discount for paying 30 days early. You can borrow cheaply. How do you decide?

A 1% discount for 30 days is about 12% a year. Comparing a 30-day rate with an annual one misleads.

3A large buyer wants much longer supplier terms without crushing hundreds of small suppliers. Which tool fits best?

Suppliers are paid early at the buyer’s credit; the buyer keeps long terms. Discounting spends own cash; factoring sells receivables.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Lean stock and longer supplier terms release cash but spend resilience: shelves empty faster, and stretched suppliers price it back or fail. Yet every surplus pallet is idle cash. Which stock earns its keep?

🧩 When the rule breaks

Some stock is strategic: critical spares and long-lead imports justify far more days. Ramadan and Eid build-ups are planned, not bloated. Supplier finance that mainly extends your terms can look debt-like to rating agencies.

🏢 In real companies

The best programmes join finance, supply chain and procurement. Many GCC firms import over long sea routes, so better demand planning beats blanket stock cuts. Regional banks offer supply-chain finance, helping large buyers support small local suppliers.

💼 In an interview

“Inventory has grown faster than sales. What would you do?”

Treat inventory as trapped capital. I would segment stock by turn and margin, cut the slow movers, and reset reorder points with operations so we do not starve sales. On payables, I would extend terms only where suppliers do not price it back through lost discounts. The goal is a shorter cash cycle through efficiency, not extremes that break the supply chain.

📖 Key terms
Safety stock
The buffer held against demand spikes and late deliveries.
Supply-chain finance
A bank pays suppliers early at the buyer’s credit rate.
Dynamic discounting
The buyer pays early from its own cash for a discount.
