Cash Stuck in Stock

Cash-conversion cycle · supplier financing

6 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

How to free cash stuck in surplus stock and supplier terms without starving sales or souring suppliers.

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Stock is cash you have already spent and not yet earned back.
  • Cut slow movers, not safety stock.
  • Supplier credit is only free until someone prices it back.
  • Skipping a 2/10 net 45 discount costs about 21% a year.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Size the cash and yearly capital charge released by cutting excess stock days.
  • Calculate the true yearly cost of skipping an early-payment discount.
  • Choose between supply-chain finance, dynamic discounting and factoring.

💡The idea

Think of a family pantry. Enough food to reach the next shop, plus a little for surprises, is good planning. Six months of tins is not safety — it is money on a shelf, creeping towards its use-by date and taking up space you pay for.

Keep the safety stock (the buffer against surprises) on fast sellers; cut slow movers nobody is waiting for. On suppliers, pay later only where it is free. ‘2/10 net 45’ means 2% off if you pay within 10 days, else full price by day 45 — skipping it is borrowing at over 20% a year.

🧮The maths

Cash released from stock

Cash released = days of stock removed × daily cost of sales

days of stock (DIO)
inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365
daily cost of sales
annual cost of sales ÷ 365

Annual cost of skipping an early-payment discount

Cost = d ÷ (1 − d) × 365 ÷ (N − D)

d
discount for paying early (2% = 0.02)
N
final due date, in days
D
last day to take the discount

✍️Worked example: The overstocked distributor

A distributor has daily sales of SAR 3.0M and daily cost of sales of SAR 2.25M (821.25 ÷ 365). It holds 82 days of stock, collects in 61 and pays in 45. One supplier offers 2/10 net 45, which it takes. WACC is 8%.

The working, step by step
  1. Cycle today = 82 + 61 − 45 = 98 days. The extra stock is mostly slow movers.
  2. Cut stock to 50 days: 32 × SAR 2.25M = SAR 72M released; 72 × 8% = SAR 5.76M a year saved.
  3. Skip the discount? Cost = 2/98 × 365/35 ≈ 21.3% a year — far above the 8% WACC. Keep taking it.
  4. Stretch suppliers without discounts from 45 to 55 days: 10 × SAR 2.25M = SAR 22.5M; 22.5 × 8% = SAR 1.8M a year.
  5. New cycle = 50 + 61 − 55 = 56 days. Released 72 + 22.5 = SAR 94.5M; saved 5.76 + 1.8 = SAR 7.56M a year.

Answer. Three-quarters of the cash sits in the warehouse. Stretch suppliers only where the days are free — a 21% discount beats an 8% WACC.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • You inherit a bloated balance sheet: sliders open at 61 days to collect, 82 of stock, 45 to pay. Leaving them is a D.
  • ‘Hold inventory’ is the big lever: each day removed frees a day of cost of sales, lifting EVA by that cash × WACC.
  • Pushback starts below about 35 days of stock or 40 to collect, or beyond 60 to pay, and grows fast. Stop near each line.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Cutting every product line by the same percentage

✓ Segment by turnover: cut slow movers hard and protect safety stock on fast sellers.

✗ Skipping early-payment discounts to flatter supplier days

✓ Annualise each discount; if it beats your WACC or borrowing rate, pay early.

✗ Counting only the capital charge on surplus stock

✓ Add storage, insurance, handling, shrinkage and obsolescence: the true carrying cost is higher.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1A retailer holds 90 days of stock; peers hold 60. Daily cost of sales is SAR 4M and WACC is 10%. If it matches peers without losing sales, what does it gain?

30 excess days × SAR 4M = SAR 120M, released once; at 10% that saves SAR 12M a year. It is not profit, and the business still needs its 60 days.

2A supplier offers 1/10 net 40. Your company can borrow at 7%. What should you do?

1/99 × 365/30 ≈ 12.3% a year. Comparing 1% with 7% mixes a 30-day rate with an annual one. Borrowing at 7% to pay early still gains about 5 points.

3A large buyer wants to move hundreds of small suppliers from 60-day to 120-day terms without causing them a cash crunch. Which tool fits best?

Supply-chain finance pays suppliers early at the buyer’s cheaper credit while the buyer keeps 120 days. Dynamic discounting spends the buyer’s own cash; factoring concerns customers, not suppliers.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Lean stock and longer supplier terms release cash but spend resilience: a late ship becomes empty shelves, and stretched suppliers price it back or fail. Yet every surplus pallet is idle cash that could repay debt. The question: which stock earns its keep?

🧩 When the rule breaks

Some stock is strategic: critical spares and long-lead imports can justify far more days. Build-ups before Ramadan and Eid are planned, not bloated. And supplier-finance programmes that mainly extend the buyer’s terms can be treated as debt-like by rating agencies and auditors.

🏢 In real companies

The best programmes are run jointly by finance, supply chain and procurement. Many GCC firms import inputs over long sea routes, so better demand planning beats blanket stock cuts. Retailers must unwind Ramadan and Eid peaks quickly. Regional banks offer supply-chain finance, letting large buyers support small local suppliers while lengthening their own terms.

💼 In an interview

“Inventory has grown faster than sales. What would you do?”

Treat inventory as trapped capital. I would segment stock by turn and margin, cut the slow movers, and reset reorder points with operations so we do not starve sales. On payables, I would extend terms only where suppliers do not price it back through lost discounts. The goal is a shorter cash cycle through efficiency, not extremes that break the supply chain.

📖 Key terms
Carrying cost
Capital charge plus storage, insurance, handling, shrinkage and obsolescence.
Safety stock
The buffer held against demand spikes and late deliveries.
Supply-chain finance
A bank pays suppliers early at the buyer’s credit rate; the buyer pays later.
Dynamic discounting
The buyer pays early from its own cash in return for a discount.
Factoring
Selling your receivables to a financier for cash today.
