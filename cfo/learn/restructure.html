One Quarter From the Edge

Financial distress · covenant management

3 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Over-levered and one weak quarter from a covenant breach: how far and how fast do you cut debt?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Covenants turn a bad quarter into the lender’s decision.
  • Far past the sweet spot, cutting debt adds value.
  • Companies fail when cash runs out: protect liquidity.

💡The idea

On a mountain road, a metre from the edge feels fine on a clear day. In fog or ice, it is one skid from disaster. Leverage is your distance from the edge; a bad quarter is the skid.

Covenants, such as maximum net debt ÷ EBITDA, let lenders act while you are still profitable: raise rates, block dividends or call the debt. Far past the sweet spot, cutting debt lowers WACC and risk. Act early, while you set the terms, and keep cash.

🧭Decide like a CFO: Seven per cent from a breach

Leverage sits just under the covenant, with about 7% EBITDA headroom, and a weak quarter looms. Selling a non-core business at a healthy price could repay debt.

Wait and hope → likely breach; lenders can re-price debt, block dividends or call the debt.

Repay debt with cash in hand → net debt unchanged, liquidity drained, covenant no safer.

Sell the non-core business now → headroom widens; a little EBITDA lost, breach avoided.

The call. Sell before the quarter. Losing a little profit is far cheaper than a breach that re-prices all the debt.

🧮The formula, in plain words

Covenant headroom

EBITDA headroom = 1 − (Actual leverage ÷ Covenant maximum)

The share of EBITDA you can lose before lenders gain the right to act.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Waiting for the test date to see if you breach.

✓ Forecast covenant tests under a downside case; talk to lenders early.

✗ A token cut that leaves you still junk-rated.

✓ Cut far enough that one bad quarter cannot trigger a breach.

✗ Using every riyal of cash to repay debt.

✓ That leaves net debt unchanged. Keep a buffer for suppliers and staff.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1To cut debt, the treasurer proposes using almost all the company’s cash to repay loans. What is the main flaw?

Net debt subtracts cash, so the covenant ratio does not move. Companies fail when cash runs out.

2Your downside forecast shows a covenant breach in two quarters. What should you do first?

Early, you negotiate as a credible borrower with choices. After a breach, lenders hold the rights.

3At 78% debt and CCC, a colleague says: “Repaying debt loses the tax shield, so it destroys value.” Your reply?

The shield wins only up to the sweet spot. Far beyond it, cutting debt lowers WACC and adds value.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Deleveraging has costs: you lose interest tax savings, sold assets take their profits, new equity dilutes owners, and dividend cuts disappoint. Not deleveraging can cost more: a breach can re-price debt and hand lenders control.

🧩 When the rule breaks

Safe leverage depends on cash-flow stability: contracted income supports more debt than cyclical sales. Covenants are negotiable; lenders often prefer a paid waiver to a default. And cutting too far wastes cheap capital.

🏢 In real companies

Gulf bank facilities, Murabaha and sukuk carry covenants like conventional loans. Most problems are solved privately: waivers and extensions with relationship banks. Saudi Arabia’s 2018 Bankruptcy Law offers reorganisation if that fails. The dollar peg passes US rate rises through.

💼 In an interview

“We are close to breaching a loan covenant. What do you do?”

Act before the breach, not after. I would forecast the covenant tests for the next four quarters, open an early conversation with lenders, and prepare options: deleverage with asset sales or cash conservation, renegotiate terms or seek a waiver with a credible plan. In distress, survival comes before optimising the tax shield; early deleveraging buys the room to keep operating.

📖 Key terms
Financial covenant
A loan or sukuk test, like maximum net debt ÷ EBITDA.
Headroom
How far results can worsen before a covenant is breached.
Waiver
A lender agreeing to overlook a breach, usually for a fee.
