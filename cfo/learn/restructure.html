One Quarter From the Edge

Financial distress · covenant management

5 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Over-levered and one weak quarter from a covenant breach: how far and how fast should you cut debt?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Covenants turn a bad quarter into the lender’s decision, not yours.
  • Measure headroom: how far can EBITDA fall before you breach?
  • Far past the sweet spot, cutting debt lowers cost of capital and risk.
  • Companies fail when cash runs out. Protect liquidity while you cut debt.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Calculate covenant headroom: how far EBITDA can fall before a breach.

💡The idea

On a mountain road, a metre from the edge feels fine on a clear day. In fog or ice, that metre is one skid from disaster. Leverage is your distance from the edge; a bad quarter is the skid. Covenants move the edge closer: lenders can act while you are still profitable.

A covenant is a test in the loan or sukuk agreement, such as maximum net debt ÷ EBITDA. Fail it and lenders can raise rates, block dividends or call the debt. Far past the sweet spot, cutting debt lowers WACC (the cost of capital) and risk together. Act early, while you choose the terms, and never empty the bank account.

🧮The maths

Net leverage

Leverage = Net debt ÷ EBITDA

Net debt
borrowings minus cash
EBITDA
operating earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation

Covenant headroom

EBITDA headroom = 1 − (Actual leverage ÷ Covenant maximum)

EBITDA headroom
share EBITDA can fall before the test fails
Actual leverage
today’s net debt ÷ EBITDA
Covenant maximum
highest ratio the loan agreement allows

✍️Worked example: Seven per cent from a breach

Net debt SAR 1,300M at 7%, EBITDA SAR 400M, covenant maximum 3.5×. A weak quarter could cut EBITDA 15%. Option: sell a non-core business for SAR 250M (SAR 20M of EBITDA) and repay debt.

The working, step by step
  1. Today: 1,300 ÷ 400 = 3.25×. Headroom = 1 − 3.25 ÷ 3.5 = 7.1%: only a SAR 28.6M EBITDA drop.
  2. Weak quarter: EBITDA 400 × 0.85 = 340; leverage 1,300 ÷ 340 ≈ 3.82×. Breach.
  3. After the sale: net debt 1,300 − 250 = 1,050, EBITDA 380; 1,050 ÷ 380 ≈ 2.76×, headroom ≈ 21%.
  4. Same weak quarter: 380 × 0.85 = 323; 1,050 ÷ 323 ≈ 3.25×. Passed.
  5. The cost: EBITDA lost 20 minus interest saved 250 × 7% = 17.5, so pre-tax earnings fall SAR 2.5M a year.

Answer. Sell before the quarter: headroom triples from 7% to 21% for SAR 2.5M a year. A breach adding 2 points on SAR 1,300M costs 26M.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Waiting for the test date to see if you breach.

✓ Forecast four quarters of covenant tests under a downside case; talk to lenders early, with a plan.

✗ A token cut that leaves the company still junk-rated.

✓ Cut far enough that one bad quarter cannot trigger a breach.

✗ Using every riyal of cash to repay debt.

✓ Repaying with cash leaves net debt unchanged. Keep a buffer to pay suppliers and staff.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1Net debt is SAR 900M, EBITDA SAR 300M, and the covenant caps net debt ÷ EBITDA at 3.5×. Roughly how far can EBITDA fall before a breach?

Leverage is 900 ÷ 300 = 3.0×. Headroom = 1 − 3.0 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 14.3%. 16.7% divides the gap by today’s ratio, not the limit.

2Your downside forecast shows a covenant breach in two quarters. What should you do first?

Early, you negotiate as a credible borrower with choices; after a breach, lenders hold the rights. Drawing the facility does not change net debt, and higher payouts weaken the ratios.

3At 78% debt and CCC, a colleague says: “Paying down debt gives up the tax shield, so it must reduce value.” What is wrong with this?

The shield wins only up to the sweet spot. At 78%, WACC is about 11.8% against 8.25% near 47% debt, so deleveraging adds value. The shield is real, just outweighed.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Deleveraging costs something: you lose the tax saving on interest, sold assets take their profits, new equity dilutes owners, and dividend cuts disappoint them. Not deleveraging can cost more: a breach can re-price all the debt and hand lenders control.

🧩 When the rule breaks

Safe leverage depends on cash-flow stability: steady contracted income supports more debt than cyclical sales. Covenants are negotiable; lenders often prefer a paid waiver to a default. Loan-agreement “adjusted EBITDA” may differ from the accounts. And cutting too far wastes cheap capital.

🏢 In real companies

In the Gulf, bank facilities, Murabaha and sukuk carry covenants just like conventional loans. Most problems are solved privately through waivers, amendments and maturity extensions with relationship banks. If that fails, Saudi Arabia’s 2018 Bankruptcy Law offers protective settlement and financial reorganisation. The riyal’s dollar peg means US rate rises can squeeze Saudi interest cover.

💼 In an interview

“We are close to breaching a loan covenant. What do you do?”

Act before the breach, not after. I would forecast the covenant tests for the next four quarters, open an early conversation with lenders, and prepare options: deleverage with asset sales or cash conservation, renegotiate terms or seek a waiver with a credible plan. In distress, survival comes before optimising the tax shield; early deleveraging buys the room to keep operating.

📖 Key terms
Financial covenant
A loan or sukuk test, like maximum net debt ÷ EBITDA, checked each period.
Headroom
How far results can worsen before a covenant is breached.
Waiver
A lender agreeing to overlook a breach, usually for a fee.
Deleveraging
Cutting debt relative to earnings: repaying debt, raising equity or growing earnings.
Liquidity
Cash available to pay bills when due; distinct from solvency.
