The Leverage Line

Trade-off theory of capital structure

6 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

How much debt to use: enough to capture cheap, tax-shielded money, not so much that the rating breaks.

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Debt is cheap until the rating breaks.
  • The WACC curve is flat at the bottom and a cliff on the right.
  • Size debt for the bad year, not the average one.
  • All-equity is not safe and free — it is safe and expensive.

💡The idea

Think of a tower built floor by floor. Debt floors are cheap, so it rises fast, but each one adds height in the wind. Too short wastes cheap floors; too tall is fine on calm days and terrifying in a storm. Steady ground — contracted cash flows — lets you build higher.

Debt is cheaper than owners’ money (equity): lenders are paid first, and interest is tax-deductible. So adding debt lowers WACC, the blended cost of capital. But more debt cuts interest cover (profit ÷ interest), the rating slips, and everyone charges more. The curve is flat at the bottom and a cliff on the right: aim slightly safe.

🧮The maths

Weighted average cost of capital

WACC = E/V × Ke + D/V × Kd × (1 − t)

E/V, D/V
shares of equity and debt in total capital
Ke
cost of equity: the return owners require
Kd
cost of debt: the rate lenders charge
t
tax rate on profits

Interest cover

Interest cover = EBIT ÷ interest

EBIT
operating profit, before interest and tax
interest
annual interest cost

✍️Worked example: Three ways to fund SAR 2bn

Capital is SAR 2,000M, EBIT SAR 250M, tax 20%. Options: 20% debt (AA; debt 4.6%, equity 9.8%), 40% debt (A; 5.0%, 11.2%) or 65% debt (B; 8.6%, 15.0%).

The working, step by step
  1. 20% debt: WACC = 0.8 × 9.8% + 0.2 × 4.6% × 0.8 = 7.84% + 0.736% = 8.576%.
  2. 40% debt: WACC = 0.6 × 11.2% + 0.4 × 5.0% × 0.8 = 6.72% + 1.60% = 8.32%.
  3. 65% debt: WACC = 0.35 × 15.0% + 0.65 × 8.6% × 0.8 = 5.25% + 4.472% = 9.722%.
  4. Interest at 40%: 800 × 5.0% = SAR 40M, cover 6.25×. At 65%: 1,300 × 8.6% = SAR 111.8M, cover ≈ 2.2×.
  5. Capital charge: SAR 171.52M, 166.4M and 194.44M a year. Tax shield: 40 × 20% = SAR 8M; 111.8 × 20% = SAR 22.36M.

Answer. 40% wins: SAR 5.12M a year cheaper than 20%, SAR 28.04M cheaper than 65%. The extra SAR 14.36M shield cannot buy back the rating.

  • Calm best: just before cover drops below 5×, usually mid-40s; a point or two past can mean a C. With the storm: about 30%.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Assuming debt is always cheaper because Kd is below Ke

✓ More debt raises the cost of equity, then of debt; judge the whole WACC.

✗ Sizing debt on this year’s profit

✓ Stress-test cover and rating in a downturn and a rate shock; size for the bad year.

✗ Assuming every riyal of interest saves tax

✓ Check the real tax position: losses, zakat and tax holidays all change the shield.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1A company with 20% debt is rated AA. Raising debt to 40% for a buyback would cut the rating to A, still investment grade. Profit is stable. What happens to WACC?

At moderate leverage the tax shield and cheap debt outweigh the extra risk. The cost of equity actually rises with debt. ‘Mix never matters’ needs no taxes and no distress.

2Moving from 40% to 65% debt nearly triples the tax shield, yet WACC rises from 8.3% to 9.7%. Why?

The shield is real cash, but the rating drops, all debt re-prices and owners demand more. Past the low point, each extra riyal of shield costs more than it saves.

3Two companies are identical, except one has steady, contracted cash flows and the other volatile, commodity-linked earnings. Which fits trade-off theory?

Distress risk is the chance cash falls short of interest. Steady cash makes that less likely, so the optimum moves right. Refusing all debt gives up cheap value.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Debt buys a lower WACC with flexibility: it uses up headroom for downturns and rate shocks, and covenants restrict dividends. Too safe has a quiet cost: an all-equity company pays a higher WACC every year. Safety is not free; it is paid out of sight.

🧩 When the rule breaks

Trade-off theory explains the shape, not the number. Firms often prefer internal cash, then debt, then equity. Loss-makers gain little tax shield. Stable utilities can carry leverage that would sink a cyclical tech firm. Ratings are judgements; the game simplifies them into clean bands.

🏢 In real companies

Boards usually set a target rating or a ratio like net debt to EBITDA. Much GCC borrowing is Sharia-compliant (sukuk, murabaha), but the logic holds: a fixed obligation due in bad years. Saudi Arabia levies zakat on the Saudi and GCC-owned share and income tax on the foreign-owned share, so shields differ.

💼 In an interview

“How do you decide how much debt the company should carry?”

Trade-off theory. Debt is cheaper than equity because interest is tax-deductible, so some leverage lowers WACC. But each extra notch raises the probability and cost of financial distress — rating downgrades, higher spreads, covenants. I target the range where the tax shield still outweighs distress cost, keep headroom for a downturn, and check it against our rating target and cash-flow volatility.

📖 Key terms
WACC
Weighted average cost of capital: the blended after-tax return lenders and owners require.
Tax shield
Tax saved because interest is deductible: interest × tax rate.
Interest cover
EBIT ÷ interest: how many times profit covers the interest bill.
Credit rating
An agency’s repayment grade; BBB and above is investment grade.
Trade-off theory
The best debt level balances the tax shield against rising distress costs.
