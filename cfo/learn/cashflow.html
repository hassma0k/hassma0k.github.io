Trapped Cash

Cash-conversion cycle

6 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

How long cash sits between paying suppliers and collecting from customers — and how far to shorten that gap.

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Working capital is capital: every day in the cycle is money someone funds.
  • A day off the cycle releases cash once and saves the capital charge yearly.
  • Trim the cheapest days first — the last ones cost the most.
  • Aim for the shortest cycle the business can live with.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Calculate the cash-conversion cycle: DIO + DSO − DPO.
  • Turn days saved into cash released and yearly capital charge saved.
  • Choose which lever to pull first and spot where pushback starts.

💡The idea

Think of your cash as a boomerang. It leaves your hand when you pay a supplier and comes back when a customer pays you. The flight time is the cash-conversion cycle. While it flies, you cannot use it — and the more you sell, the more boomerangs you need in the air.

Cycle = days stock sits (DIO) + days customers take to pay (DSO) − days you take to pay suppliers (DPO). Each day removed releases cash once; that cash then saves the capital charge every year. But the last days are dear: customers, stockouts and suppliers push back. Aim for the shortest cycle the business can live with.

🧮The maths

Cash-conversion cycle

CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO

DIO
days stock sits: inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365
DSO
days customers take to pay: receivables ÷ revenue × 365
DPO
days to pay suppliers: payables ÷ cost of sales × 365

Cash released

Cash released = ΔDSO × daily sales + (ΔDIO + ΔDPO) × daily cost of sales

ΔDSO, ΔDIO
days cut from collection and from stock
ΔDPO
days added to supplier payment
daily sales
annual revenue ÷ 365
daily cost of sales
annual cost of sales ÷ 365

✍️Worked example: Halving a wholesaler’s cycle

A wholesaler sells SAR 730M a year (SAR 2.0M a day) with cost of sales of SAR 547.5M (SAR 1.5M a day). It holds 50 days of stock, collects in 60 and pays in 40. Target: 40, 45 and 50. WACC is 9%.

The working, step by step
  1. Cycle before = 50 + 60 − 40 = 70 days. Cycle after = 40 + 45 − 50 = 35 days.
  2. Collections: 15 days × SAR 2.0M daily sales = SAR 30M released.
  3. Stock: 10 days × SAR 1.5M daily cost = SAR 15M. Suppliers: 10 extra days × SAR 1.5M = SAR 15M.
  4. Total released = 30 + 15 + 15 = SAR 60M. Capital charge saved = 60 × 9% = SAR 5.4M a year.

Answer. SAR 60M released once, and EVA up SAR 5.4M every year with profit unchanged. Stock and supplier days are worth daily cost, not daily sales.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Treating profit as cash

✓ Read cash flow and the cycle beside profit; rising profit with a longer cycle can drain cash.

✗ Squeezing one lever to the limit

✓ Trim the cheapest days on each leg first; the last days on any leg cost most.

✗ Stretching suppliers by default

✓ Price it first: lost discounts or higher prices often cost more than the WACC saved.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1Daily sales are SAR 1M. DSO falls from 55 to 45 days; nothing else changes. WACC is 10%. What happens?

10 days × SAR 1M = SAR 10M released once; at 10% WACC that saves about SAR 1M a year. Profit is unchanged — no new sales were made.

2The cycle is 95 days. Customers pay in 70 days on 30-day terms; peers collect in 45. Stock matches peers. Suppliers are already paid at 60 days and complaining. Where to start?

Collections are furthest out of line: 40 days past terms is cash already earned. Suppliers are at their limit, stock is healthy, and borrowing just pays interest.

3A CFO pays suppliers 20 days later. Purchases are SAR 1M a day (SAR 365M a year) and WACC is 9%. Suppliers raise prices 1%. Was it worth it?

SAR 20M is a one-off release worth 20 × 9% = SAR 1.8M a year; the price rise costs 1% × 365 = SAR 3.65M every year. EVA falls about SAR 1.85M.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Each day released costs something: pressed customers may leave, leaner stock thins your buffer, longer supplier terms can cost discounts. Cash left in the cycle carries the WACC. Buy the cheapest days first; stop when the next one costs more than it saves.

🧩 When the rule breaks

The cycle is an average. A retailer stocking up before Ramadan looks bloated one quarter and lean the next, so compare like with like. Year-end figures are easy to flatter. And growth needs more working capital even if the cycle stays the same.

🏢 In real companies

Grocers collect at the till and pay suppliers weeks later; contractors can wait months for milestone approvals. In Saudi Arabia and the GCC, long approval chains make collections discipline a core skill. With the riyal pegged to the dollar, local rates tend to track US ones, so a rate rise makes every day dearer.

💼 In an interview

“The company is profitable but short of cash. What do you look at first?”

The cash-conversion cycle: DSO + DIO − DPO. Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. I would find which leg is out of line against peers, then act on the cheapest one first — usually collections and excess stock, before stretching suppliers. Every day removed releases cash that no longer carries the cost of capital, as long as customers and suppliers are not pushed so hard they push back.

📖 Key terms
Cash-conversion cycle (CCC)
Days between paying for stock and collecting cash: DIO + DSO − DPO.
Working capital
Receivables plus stock minus payables: cash tied up in daily trading.
WACC
Weighted average cost of capital: the yearly return lenders and owners expect.
Capital charge
Capital × WACC: the yearly cost of the money tied up.
EVA
Economic value added: operating profit after tax minus the capital charge.
