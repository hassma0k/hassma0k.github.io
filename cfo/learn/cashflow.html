Trapped Cash

Cash-conversion cycle

3 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

How short should the cash cycle be, and which lever do you pull first?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Every day in the cycle is money someone funds.
  • A day saved releases cash once and saves capital charge yearly.
  • Buy the cheapest days first; the last ones cost most.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Explain why every day in the cycle costs value.
  • Weigh collections, stock and supplier levers against their pushback.
  • Judge where to stop before customers and suppliers push back.

💡The idea

Think of cash as a boomerang. It leaves when you pay a supplier and returns when a customer pays you. While it flies you cannot use it, and the more you sell, the more boomerangs are in the air.

Every day in the cycle is money someone funds at your WACC. Cutting a day releases cash once and saves its capital charge every year, lifting EVA with profit unchanged. But the last days cost most: customers, stock buffers and suppliers push back.

🧭Decide like a CFO: Where to find the cash

A wholesaler’s cycle is 70 days against peers at about 40. Customers pay well past terms, stock is slightly heavy, and key suppliers already complain about payment times. The board wants cash released.

Fix billing, disputes and chasing → earned cash comes home, with little customer risk.

Trim slow-moving stock → frees cash, but cut too deep and stockouts lose sales.

Stretch suppliers → quick cash, but price rises or lost discounts can cost more than the capital charge saved.

The call. Collections first, slow stock second, suppliers last. Buy the cheapest days first; stop when the next day costs more than it saves.

🧮The formula, in plain words

Cash-conversion cycle

CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO

Days stock sits, plus days customers take to pay, minus days you take to pay suppliers.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • Three sliders: collect (20–90 days), inventory (15–90) and pay suppliers (20–95); a run opens at 61, 58, 45.
  • Pushback starts below about 40 days to collect or 35 of stock, or beyond 60 to pay.
  • Each lever at its comfort line usually earns an A; untouched sliders score a D.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Treating profit as cash

✓ Read the cycle beside profit; rising profit with a longer cycle drains cash.

✗ Squeezing one lever to its limit

✓ Trim the cheapest days on each lever; the last days cost most.

✗ Stretching suppliers by default

✓ Price it: lost discounts or price rises often exceed the capital charge saved.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1Collections drop ten days with no change in sales. How should you describe the impact to the board?

The release is one-off cash, not profit; the lower balance then saves its capital charge every year it stays down.

2Customers pay far past terms, stock matches peers, and suppliers already complain about late payment. Where do you start?

Overdue receivables are cash already earned. Suppliers are at their limit, stock is healthy, and borrowing only adds interest.

3Paying suppliers later releases cash, but they respond by raising prices. When is the stretch still worth it?

Compare like with like: the release saves capital charge each year; the price rise costs each year. Lump sum versus yearly misleads.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Each day released spends something: pressed customers may leave, leaner stock thins the buffer, longer supplier terms can cost discounts or goodwill. Cash left in the cycle carries the WACC; balance the two.

🧩 When the rule breaks

The cycle is an average. Pre-Ramadan stock builds look bloated one quarter and lean the next, so compare like with like. Year-end figures are easy to flatter. Growth still needs more working capital.

🏢 In real companies

Grocers collect at the till; contractors can wait months for milestone approvals. In Saudi Arabia and the GCC, long approval chains make collections a core skill. With the riyal pegged to the dollar, US rate rises make every day dearer.

💼 In an interview

“The company is profitable but short of cash. What do you look at first?”

The cash-conversion cycle: DSO + DIO − DPO. Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. I would find which leg is out of line against peers, then act on the cheapest one first — usually collections and excess stock, before stretching suppliers. Every day removed releases cash that no longer carries the cost of capital, as long as customers and suppliers are not pushed so hard they push back.

📖 Key terms
Cash-conversion cycle (CCC)
Days between paying for stock and collecting cash from customers.
Capital charge
Capital tied up × WACC: its yearly cost.
EVA
Economic value added: operating profit after tax minus capital charge.
