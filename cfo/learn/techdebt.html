The Debt You Can’t See

Technical debt as fragile leverage

6 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Old systems are cheap in calm times and amplify shocks: how much debt suits a fragile company, and when to fix them?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Technical debt is leverage you cannot see on the balance sheet.
  • Calm years hide fragility; storms reveal it.
  • Price the hidden interest: carrying cost plus expected cost of failure.
  • Fragile systems and heavy borrowing compound. Do not stack one on the other.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Explain why technical debt behaves like leverage: cheap in calm, harsh in shocks.
  • Price technical debt, including expected failure cost, and test the fix against the hurdle.
  • Set less debt than the calm-weather optimum when operations are fragile.

💡The idea

Think of an old bridge rated for everyday traffic. It carries cars for years without trouble, and nobody learns its real limit until the flood or the heavy lorry arrives. Ageing systems are the same: cheap while nothing changes, then a demand spike, new regulation or cyber incident turns into an outage.

Technical debt is the cost of shortcuts and ageing systems. You pay interest every year in maintenance and workarounds, plus the expected cost of failure. It behaves like leverage and is invisible on the balance sheet. So treat fixing it as an investment that must clear the hurdle, and carry less financial debt until you do.

🧮The maths

Interest rate on technical debt

Tech-debt rate = (Annual carrying cost + Expected failure cost) ÷ Cost to fix

Annual carrying cost
extra maintenance, workarounds and slow change, every year
Expected failure cost
chance of failure per year × cost of a failure
Cost to fix
one-off modernisation cost: the principal

Value of paying it down

EVA = (Tech-debt rate − WACC) × Cost to fix

Tech-debt rate
annual cost you stop paying, as a return
WACC
the hurdle rate
Cost to fix
capital the modernisation ties up

✍️Worked example: Pricing an ageing order system

A 15-year-old order system costs SAR 60M to replace. Keeping it costs SAR 6M a year in maintenance and workarounds. There is a 20% yearly chance of a serious failure costing SAR 30M. WACC is 8.4%.

The working, step by step
  1. Visible interest: 6 ÷ 60 = 10% a year, the only cost in a normal year’s accounts.
  2. Hidden interest: 20% × 30 = SAR 6M a year. All-in rate: (6 + 6) ÷ 60 = 20%.
  3. Calm-year view: EVA = 60 × (10% − 8.4%) = SAR 0.96M. Looks marginal, so it slips to next year.
  4. Full view: EVA = 60 × (20% − 8.4%) = SAR 6.96M a year, clearly worth doing.
  5. Leverage effect: a calm year costs 6; a failure year costs 6 + 30 = 36, six times as much.

Answer. Priced fully, the fix earns 20% against an 8.4% hurdle, adding about SAR 7M a year. Until it is fixed, carry less debt.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • Tower engine from 38% debt. In calm weather legacy systems are invisible: the curve matches a modern company’s, best near 47% debt.
  • In a storm, brittle systems make it 1.6× harsher: rates jump 80, 160 or 240 basis points by difficulty, and the rating drops a notch.
  • On Medium in a brittle storm, keeping 47% scores a D; about 20–38% scores an A or B. On Hard, even 38% is a D.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Treating a clean incident record as proof of safety.

✓ Test under stress: failure drills, load tests, recovery exercises. Then price the expected failure cost.

✗ Counting only the visible maintenance bill.

✓ Add expected failure cost and slow change; the hidden interest often matches the visible part.

✗ Setting debt as if operations were robust.

✓ With brittle systems, borrow less than the calm-weather optimum so one shock cannot hit operations and covenants together.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1An IT director says: “Our core platform has had no major outage in three years, so its technical debt is harmless.” What is the strongest response?

Like leverage, technical debt does little harm until a shock. Replacing by age alone skips the value question, and the risk lands in the finance numbers.

2Fixing a legacy system costs SAR 40M. Living with it costs SAR 4M a year, plus a 10% yearly chance of a SAR 20M failure. WACC is 9%. Annual EVA of fixing?

Expected failure cost is 10% × 20 = 2M, so all-in interest is 6M on 40M, 15%. 40 × (15% − 9%) = 2.4M. Avoided costs are its return.

3Two companies have identical calm-weather WACC curves, but one runs brittle legacy systems that make a storm hit 1.6× harder. Where should the brittle company set its debt?

Identical calm curves are what hide the risk. In the game, the brittle storm optimum is about 26% against 47% calm. Zero debt goes too far: WACC is higher there.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Holding about 26% debt instead of 47% raises calm-weather WACC by roughly a quarter of a point. Fixing systems uses capital and your best engineers’ time. A big replacement can itself cause an outage. You choose between an insurance premium and a rare, huge bill.

🧩 When the rule breaks

Failure probabilities are rough, and if one failure could sink the company, resilience is worth more than the arithmetic shows. A system switched off next year may not be worth fixing. Sometimes wrapping a legacy system beats replacing it. Cash, insurance and redundancy also help.

🏢 In real companies

Gulf banks, insurers, utilities and government-linked entities often run core systems extended for decades. In Saudi Arabia, ZATCA’s Phase-2 e-invoicing prompted many invoicing and ERP upgrades, and the National Cybersecurity Authority sets controls old systems struggle to meet. Keep a technical-debt register beside the debt schedule, with an owner, interest cost and pay-down plan.

💼 In an interview

“Why should a CFO care about technical debt?”

Because it behaves like hidden leverage. Old systems keep costs low in calm conditions but magnify losses when something breaks — outages, security incidents, slow responses to change. I would quantify the risk like financial leverage, budget steady pay-down, and run a more conservative overall risk profile while the debt is high, rather than optimising for the calm-weather case.

📖 Key terms
Technical debt
Future cost of shortcuts and ageing systems that make change slower and riskier.
Principal (of technical debt)
The one-off cost of fixing or modernising the system.
Interest (of technical debt)
The recurring cost: maintenance, workarounds, slow change and expected failures.
Expected loss
Probability of an event multiplied by its cost if it happens.
Hidden leverage
Anything that lowers costs in normal times but magnifies losses in a shock.
