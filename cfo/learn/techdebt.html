The Debt You Can’t See

Technical debt as fragile leverage

3 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Old systems are cheap until a shock hits: when to fix them, and how much to borrow?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Technical debt is leverage hidden from the balance sheet.
  • Price hidden interest: upkeep plus expected cost of failure.
  • Do not stack heavy borrowing on fragile systems.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Explain why technical debt behaves like hidden leverage.
  • Judge a fix against the full cost it removes.
  • Weigh borrowing less while operations remain fragile.

💡The idea

Think of an old bridge rated for everyday traffic. It carries cars for years without trouble; nobody learns its real limit until the flood arrives. Ageing systems are the same: cheap until a demand spike, new regulation or cyber incident.

Technical debt is the cost of shortcuts and ageing systems. You pay interest yearly in maintenance, workarounds and expected failures, yet it never appears on the balance sheet. Treat fixing it as an investment that must clear the hurdle, and borrow less until you do.

🧭Decide like a CFO: An ageing order system

A 15-year-old order system works, but upkeep is high and a serious failure is a real yearly risk. Replacing it costs SAR 60M. The board asks: fix now?

Judge on visible upkeep only → looks marginal, slips to next year, failure risk stays unpriced.

Price in expected failure cost → the fix’s return rises and clearly beats the hurdle.

Keep it and borrow heavily → a failure year hits operations and covenants together.

The call. Fund the replacement: fully priced, it clears the hurdle comfortably. Until it is live, carry less debt than the calm-weather optimum.

🧮The formula, in plain words

Interest rate on technical debt

Tech-debt rate = (Annual carrying cost + Expected failure cost) ÷ Cost to fix

What the old system really costs each year, including likely failures, per riyal needed to fix it.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • Tower engine from 38% debt. In calm weather legacy systems are invisible; best debt is near 47%.
  • Brittle storms hit 1.6× harder: rates rise 80, 160 or 240 bp by difficulty; rating drops a notch.
  • Medium brittle storm: staying at 47% scores D; 20–38% scores A or B. Hard: even 38% is D.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Treating a clean incident record as proof of safety.

✓ Stress-test with drills, load tests and recovery exercises, then price failure.

✗ Counting only the visible maintenance bill.

✓ Add expected failure cost: probability of failure times its cost.

✗ Setting debt as if operations were robust.

✓ With brittle systems, borrow below the calm-weather optimum.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1An IT director says: “Our core platform has had no outage in three years, so its technical debt is harmless.” Strongest response?

Like leverage, technical debt does little harm until a shock. Replacing on age alone skips the value question.

2A system fix looks marginal on maintenance savings alone. What should you add before deciding?

Avoided failures are part of the return. Ignoring them makes fixes look marginal and keeps fragile systems running.

3Two companies have identical calm-weather WACC curves, but one runs brittle systems that make storms hit harder. Where should it set debt?

Identical calm curves hide the risk. Borrow below the calm optimum, but zero debt overshoots: WACC is higher there.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Holding less debt raises calm-weather WACC slightly. Fixing systems uses capital and your best engineers. A big replacement can itself cause an outage. You trade an insurance premium against a rare, huge bill.

🧩 When the rule breaks

Failure probabilities are rough guesses; if one failure could sink the company, resilience is worth more than the arithmetic shows. A system retiring soon may not deserve fixing. Sometimes wrapping legacy beats replacing it.

🏢 In real companies

Gulf banks, insurers, utilities and government-linked entities often run decades-old core systems. In Saudi Arabia, ZATCA’s Phase-2 e-invoicing prompted upgrades, and National Cybersecurity Authority controls are hard for old systems to meet. Keep a technical-debt register beside the debt schedule.

💼 In an interview

“Why should a CFO care about technical debt?”

Because it behaves like hidden leverage. Old systems keep costs low in calm conditions but magnify losses when something breaks — outages, security incidents, slow responses to change. I would quantify the risk like financial leverage, budget steady pay-down, and run a more conservative overall risk profile while the debt is high, rather than optimising for the calm-weather case.

📖 Key terms
Technical debt
Future cost of shortcuts and ageing systems that slow change.
Expected loss
Probability of an event multiplied by its cost if it happens.
Hidden leverage
Anything that cuts costs in calm times but magnifies shocks.
