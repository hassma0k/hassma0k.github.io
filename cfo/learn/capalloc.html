Fund It or Kill It

Positive-NPV rule · capital rationing

6 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Six proposals, one budget: which projects deserve scarce capital, and what should happen to the riyals you don’t spend?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Only returns above the cost of capital create value; profit alone is not enough.
  • When capital is scarce, rank by value per unit of the scarcest resource.
  • A full budget is not success. A high return on each riyal is.
  • Idle cash drags, returned cash is neutral, a below-hurdle trophy destroys value.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Test any project against the hurdle rate and say if it creates value.
  • Rank projects by value per unit of whatever is scarcest: cash or people.
  • Explain why idle cash costs value and returned cash does not.

💡The idea

Picture a high-jump bar set at the cost of capital. Each project’s return is the height of its jump. Clearing the bar by a metre beats clearing it by a centimetre. Knocking it down doesn’t score zero; it costs points. A shiny HQ tower returning 3% never leaves the ground, however good the brochure looks.

The bar is WACC (weighted average cost of capital): the blended return lenders and shareholders expect. Only returns above it create value. When good projects outnumber the budget or the team, rank by value per unit of the scarce resource. Leftover cash has three fates: invest above the bar, sit idle (a slow loss), or return it (neutral).

🧮The maths

Value a project adds each year (EVA)

EVA = (Return − WACC) × Capital invested

Return
the project’s annual return on its capital
WACC
the hurdle: what the capital costs
Capital invested
the riyals the project ties up

Profitability index

PI = PV of future cash flows ÷ Investment

PI
value per riyal invested; above 1.0 means positive NPV
PV of future cash flows
cash flows discounted at the WACC
Investment
the upfront capital

✍️Worked example: Ranking the six proposals

WACC 8.4%, budget SAR 420M, 7 effort points. Acquire rival: 260M, 15.4%, effort 5. Factory line: 120M, 12.5%, 3. Digital twin: 45M, 11.0%, 1. Solar plant: 150M, 9.6%, 3. Warehouse: 65M, 7.2%, 2. HQ tower: 90M, 3.0%, 2.

The working, step by step
  1. Spread = return − 8.4: Acquire 7.0, Factory 4.1, Twin 2.6, Solar 1.2, Warehouse −1.2, Tower −5.4. Drop the last two.
  2. EVA (SAR M a year): Acquire 260 × 7.0% = 18.20; Factory 4.92; Twin 1.17; Solar 1.80.
  3. All four need 575 against 420, and 12 points against 7. Acquire + Factory fits the cash (380) but needs 8 points.
  4. Acquire + Twin: 305, 6 points, EVA 18.20 + 1.17 = 19.37. Factory + Solar + Twin: 315, 7 points, EVA 7.89.
  5. Leftover 420 − 305 = 115. Idle at 2%: 115 × (2% − 8.4%) = −7.36 a year. Returned: 0.

Answer. Fund the acquisition and digital twin, and return the SAR 115M: SAR 19.37M of EVA a year. A full budget is not the goal.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Funding a below-hurdle project because it is “strategic”.

✓ Name what it buys. If you cannot, it loses (return − WACC) × capital every year.

✗ Funding down the list by return until money runs out.

✓ Check every limit, including people, and compare two or three complete slates.

✗ Spending the whole budget because it was approved.

✓ Idle cash at 2% against an 8.4% WACC costs 6.4% a year. Return it instead.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1WACC is 8.4%. The CEO wants a SAR 90M flagship HQ returning 3.0% a year, “to show the market we are serious”. What does it do to EVA each year?

EVA = (3.0% − 8.4%) × 90 = −4.86M. The 2.7M profit is real, but the capital costs 7.56M a year. Profit below the cost of capital destroys value.

2After funding everything above the 8.4% hurdle, SAR 115M is left. The only other proposal is a SAR 65M warehouse returning 7.2%. What is the best use of the SAR 115M?

Returning is neutral (EVA 0). The warehouse loses 65 × (7.2% − 8.4%) = 0.78M a year; idle cash loses 115 × (2% − 8.4%) = 7.36M.

3Under a hard SAR 200M budget you can fund project P (cost 200, NPV 30) or Q and R (cost 100 each, NPV 20 each). Which creates more value?

Under rationing, maximise total NPV from the budget. Q and R return 1.20 per riyal (PI) against P’s 1.15, adding 40 against 30.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Every yes is also a no: a riyal in the 9.6% solar plant is not in the 15.4% acquisition. The best slate also puts SAR 260M of 305M on one bet. Returning cash shrinks the company, and you may need to raise it again.

🧩 When the rule breaks

The rule is only as good as the return estimates, and acquisition forecasts are often the most optimistic. Riskier projects deserve a higher hurdle than one company-wide WACC. A small below-hurdle pilot can be a valuable option. There is no single correct slate.

🏢 In real companies

Real companies use an investment committee and release money in stages, so weak cases can be stopped early. In Saudi Arabia, Vision 2030 has created many large opportunities, so discipline matters most in boom years. The riyal’s dollar peg means Saudi rates, and so hurdles, tend to follow the US Federal Reserve.

💼 In an interview

“You have five good projects but money for two. How do you choose?”

First, only projects that clear our hurdle rate — the WACC adjusted for risk — create value; anything below destroys it however strategic it sounds. Then, because capital is rationed, I rank by value created per riyal invested, not by size or by who argues loudest. If nothing clears the hurdle, returning cash to shareholders beats a value-destroying trophy project.

📖 Key terms
WACC
The blended return lenders and shareholders expect: what the company’s capital costs.
Hurdle rate
The minimum return a project must earn; usually WACC, higher for riskier projects.
NPV (net present value)
Today’s value of future cash flows minus the cost; positive creates value.
Capital rationing
More value-creating projects than money or people to fund them.
Profitability index
Present value of future cash flows ÷ investment: value per riyal.
