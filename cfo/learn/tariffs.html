Landed Cost, Not Sticker Price

Total landed cost · supply-chain resilience

6 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Choose routes and suppliers on total landed cost: freight, duties, cash in transit and currency, not the freight quote.

⚡In 30 seconds

  • The cheapest freight is not the cheapest shipment.
  • Every day at sea is capital carrying your WACC.
  • Reroute around costs that follow the route; re-source around costs that follow the goods.
  • A hedge does not make money; it removes a bet you did not need.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Calculate a lane's total landed cost, including capital tied up in transit.
  • Decide when a faster, dearer route is cheaper all-in.
  • Separate route costs from goods costs, and size a currency hedge.

💡The idea

Choosing a route on the freight quote is like choosing a flight on the fare alone, ignoring baggage fees, the long connection and the hotel night. Landed cost is the full bill: goods, freight, insurance, duties, port fees, the cash tied up while goods travel, and any currency move.

Goods at sea are stock you have paid for, so each transit day carries your WACC (weighted average cost of capital). Costs that follow the route, like surcharges and longer voyages, can be escaped by rerouting; tariffs follow the goods' origin, so only re-sourcing changes them. The riyal is pegged to the dollar, so hedge euro and yuan exposure.

🧮The maths

Total landed cost

Landed cost = Goods + Freight + Duties & tariffs + Insurance & fees + Capital in transit + FX impact

Duties & tariffs
taxes on imports, set by product and origin
Capital in transit
WACC charge on bought goods still travelling
FX impact
gain or loss from moves on unhedged purchases

Capital charge in transit

Capital charge = Annual goods value × Transit days ÷ 365 × WACC

Annual goods value
value of goods moving on the lane each year
Transit days
door-to-door days, including any delay
WACC
weighted average cost of capital

✍️Worked example: Reroute, sail on, or fly?

You import SAR 365M a year (SAR 1M a day) from Europe, invoiced in euros; WACC 10%. Sea: 2% freight, 30 days. A disruption adds a 3% surcharge and 14 days. Land/rail: 4.5%, 18 days. Air: 11%, 4 days.

The working, step by step
  1. Sea now: 365 × (2% + 3%) = 18.25 freight + 44 × 1 × 10% = 4.4 capital; total 22.65.
  2. Land/rail: 365 × 4.5% = 16.425 + 18 × 1 × 10% = 1.8; total 18.225.
  3. Air: 365 × 11% = 40.15 + 4 × 1 × 10% = 0.4; total 40.55, 17.9 worse than sea.
  4. Land/rail wins: 22.65 − 18.225 = 4.425 a year cheaper than staying at sea.
  5. Currency: an unhedged 6% euro rise costs 365 × 6% = 21.9; hedging all at 1.2% costs 365 × 1.2% = 4.38.

Answer. Reroute by land/rail; skip air. Hedging is a risk decision: pay about 4.38 to remove a 21.9 swing that could go either way.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • Route three lanes (Asia, Europe, Domestic) by sea, land or air. Sea has the cheapest freight but longest transit; air the reverse, and never wins.
  • Calm quarter: sea wins every lane. A Red Sea or tariff toll (3% on Medium) tips every lane to land, except Asia on Easy.
  • Hedge imported lanes (about 1.2% premium) fully when a euro move is live, otherwise not at all. The default plan earns a D.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Choosing the lane with the lowest freight quote.

✓ Compare total landed cost, including the WACC charge on goods in transit.

✗ Rerouting to escape a tariff.

✓ Tariffs follow origin and classification; to change the duty, lawfully change the source.

✗ Switching everything to air in a disruption.

✓ Air frees little capital for its freight premium; keep it for urgent, high-value goods.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1A Saudi importer buys from a US supplier in dollars and a German supplier in euros, in similar amounts. Where should hedging attention go?

The riyal is pegged to the dollar, so dollar invoices barely move in riyal terms; the euro floats. Supplier size is irrelevant, and moves need not average out.

2A lane carries SAR 730M a year (SAR 2M a day); WACC is 8%. Sea: SAR 14.6M freight, 40 days. Land: SAR 25.55M, 15 days. Which is cheaper all-in, by how much?

Sea: 14.6 + 40 × 2 × 8% = 21.0. Land: 25.55 + 15 × 2 × 8% = 27.95. Sea wins by 6.95; 10.95 compares freight only.

3A new tariff applies to a component because of where it is made. A manager proposes trucking it through a neighbouring country to avoid it. What is the right response?

Duties depend on what the goods are and where they originate. A new path does not change origin, and disguising origin is customs fraud. Air and hedges don't touch duty.

⚖️ The trade-off

Every route trades freight against time. Fast lanes free capital but cost more; slow lanes save freight but tie up cash. Resilience (a second supplier, a reserved land route) is insurance paid in calm years. Hedging gives up favourable moves for certainty.

🧩 When the rule breaks

The maths assumes the alternative route has capacity; in a crisis everyone reroutes at once. It ignores stock-outs that lose key customers, so air is sometimes right. Tariffs can change overnight. And real treasuries hedge by policy before the move, not after seeing it.

🏢 In real companies

For Gulf importers, the Red Sea and Suez Canal are the main corridor to Europe, so disruption there means longer voyages and surcharges into Jeddah. East-coast ports and overland routes offer alternatives. Dollar imports carry little riyal risk; euro, yuan and yen invoices do. Vision 2030's local-content goals make local re-sourcing a strategic option.

💼 In an interview

“A new tariff hits our main supply route. How do you respond?”

Compare routes on total landed cost, not freight price: tariff, tolls, transit time and the working capital sitting in transit. The cheapest lane on paper may not be cheapest once delays and duties are priced in. I would reroute where the total cost is lower, keep a second source for resilience, and hedge the currency exposure so an FX move does not wipe out the saving.

📖 Key terms
Total landed cost
Everything it costs to get goods to your door, including cash and currency.
Capital in transit
Bought inventory still travelling; it carries the cost of capital.
Tariff
A tax on imported goods, set by product classification and origin.
Forward contract
An agreement to exchange currency at a fixed rate on a future date.
Currency peg
A fixed exchange rate to another currency; the riyal is pegged to the dollar.
