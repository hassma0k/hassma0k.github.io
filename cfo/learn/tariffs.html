Landed Cost, Not Sticker Price

Total landed cost · supply-chain resilience

3 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Which route and supplier really cost least once duties, transit time and currency are counted?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • The cheapest freight is not the cheapest shipment.
  • Every day at sea is capital carrying your WACC.
  • Reroute around route costs; re-source around costs that follow goods.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Judge routes on total landed cost, not freight quotes.
  • Weigh faster, dearer routes against capital tied up in transit.
  • Spot which costs rerouting escapes and which need re-sourcing.

💡The idea

Choosing a route on the freight quote is like choosing a flight on the fare alone, ignoring baggage, the long connection and the hotel night. Landed cost is the full bill, including cash tied up and currency moves.

Goods at sea are paid-for stock, so every transit day carries your cost of capital. Rerouting escapes costs that follow the route; tariffs follow the goods' origin, so only re-sourcing changes them. The riyal is pegged to the dollar; hedge euro and yuan exposure.

🧭Decide like a CFO: Reroute, sail on, or fly?

A disruption adds a surcharge and two weeks to your Europe sea lane, invoiced in euros. Land/rail costs more freight but is faster; air is fastest and dearest.

Stay at sea → cheapest base freight, but surcharge plus extra days of cash make it dearer.

Reroute by land/rail → higher quote, less cash tied up; cheapest all-in here.

Fly → frees little extra capital for a huge freight premium; the dearest option.

The call. Reroute by land/rail and skip air. Hedge the euro separately: a small cost removes a large swing either way.

🧮The formula, in plain words

Total landed cost

Landed cost = Goods + Freight + Duties & tariffs + Insurance & fees + Capital in transit + FX impact

The full bill to get goods to your door, including cash tied up and currency.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • Route three lanes (Asia, Europe, Domestic) by sea, land or air; air never wins.
  • Calm quarter: sea wins. A Red Sea or tariff toll tips lanes to land (except Asia on Easy).
  • Hedge imports fully when a euro move is live, otherwise not; the default plan earns a D.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Choosing the lane with the lowest freight quote.

✓ Compare total landed cost, including the capital charge on goods in transit.

✗ Rerouting to escape a tariff.

✓ Tariffs follow origin; to change the duty, lawfully change the source.

✗ Switching everything to air in a disruption.

✓ Air frees little capital for its premium; keep it for urgent, high-value goods.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1A Saudi importer buys similar amounts in dollars and euros. Where should hedging attention go?

The riyal is pegged to the dollar, so dollar invoices barely move in riyals; the euro floats.

2Freight on a slow sea lane is far cheaper than land, but takes weeks longer. How do you choose?

Transit days carry your cost of capital, so either route can win. Only the all-in comparison tells you which.

3A new tariff applies because of where a component is made. A manager proposes trucking it via a neighbouring country. Your response?

Duty depends on what the goods are and where they originate. A new path changes neither; disguising origin is customs fraud.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Every route trades freight against time: fast lanes free capital but cost more. Resilience, like a reserved land route, is insurance paid in calm years. Hedging trades favourable moves for certainty.

🧩 When the rule breaks

The maths assumes the other route has capacity; in a crisis everyone reroutes at once. Stock-outs that lose key customers can make air right. Tariffs change overnight, and treasuries hedge by policy, before the move.

🏢 In real companies

The Red Sea and Suez Canal are the Gulf's main corridor to Europe, so disruption means longer voyages and surcharges into Jeddah. Dollar imports carry little riyal risk; euro and yuan invoices do. Vision 2030 local-content goals make re-sourcing strategic.

💼 In an interview

“A new tariff hits our main supply route. How do you respond?”

Compare routes on total landed cost, not freight price: tariff, tolls, transit time and the working capital sitting in transit. The cheapest lane on paper may not be cheapest once delays and duties are priced in. I would reroute where the total cost is lower, keep a second source for resilience, and hedge the currency exposure so an FX move does not wipe out the saving.

📖 Key terms
Total landed cost
All it costs to land goods, including cash and currency.
Tariff
A tax on imports, set by product classification and origin.
Currency peg
A fixed exchange rate; the riyal is pegged to the dollar.
