Starve the Losers

Internal capital markets · ROIC-based allocation

3 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Several business units want capital: which earn their keep, and how hard should you starve the rest?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • Profitable is not enough: beat the cost of capital.
  • Route capital to units earning most above their hurdle.
  • Capital does not care how long you have owned a business.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Judge each unit by return on capital, not profit.
  • Weigh moving capital from weak units to strong ones.
  • Explain why equal shares and “core” loyalty destroy value.

💡The idea

Picture a household with one salary pot. One member borrows at 8.4% to earn 3.5%, works hard and proudly reports a profit. The household still gets poorer every year. A unit earning below its cost of capital does the same.

A group is an internal capital market: head office chooses who gets money, like investors choosing companies. Score units by ROIC (return on invested capital) against their hurdle. Feed the widest spreads, starve those below. Equal shares feel fair but make winners subsidise losers.

🧭Decide like a CFO: Six units, one capital round

SAR 420M to allocate and capacity to back two units well. The digital bank and logistics arm earn most above hurdle; legacy telecom, long called “core”, earns below it.

Equal shares for all → peace at head office; winners starved, losers fed, value lost.

Protect telecom plus logistics → loyalty honoured, but the group destroys value every year.

Back digital bank and logistics, return the rest → most value; telecom must fix or exit.

The call. Back the digital bank and logistics arm, refuse the below-hurdle units, and return unspent cash instead of holding it idle.

🧮The formula, in plain words

Unit EVA

EVA = (ROIC − Hurdle) × Invested capital

A unit creates value only if its return beats its own risk-adjusted hurdle on the capital it holds.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • City engine: each card is a unit’s request, showing return (as ROIC), cost, payback and effort.
  • Starving a unit means refusing its request. Idle leftover earns 2%, a drag; returning it is neutral.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Judging a unit by profit, not return on capital.

✓ Put the capital charge next to the profit before deciding.

✗ Giving every unit an equal share to keep peace.

✓ Allocate by spread above each unit’s hurdle, and publish the rule.

✗ Protecting a long-held “core” unit by default.

✓ Set a time-bound plan to fix, harvest or exit it.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1Legacy telecom’s head says: “We made a solid profit, so we deserve our capital.” Its ROIC is below the hurdle. Your view?

Profit below the capital charge destroys value. But a time-bound plan beats a rushed closure with sale discounts.

2The CEO proposes an equal share of capital for each of six units “so everyone stays motivated”. Strongest objection?

A healthy average can hide value-destroying units. Equal shares starve the best and feed the weakest.

3A riskier healthcare venture promises a return above group WACC but below its own risk-adjusted hurdle. Fund it?

Judge each unit at the hurdle matching its risk. Shareholders can diversify for themselves.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Starving a unit has costs: managers may leave, customers may notice, and the unit can lose scale. Exits bring sale discounts and separation costs. But capital left in a weak unit could earn more elsewhere.

🧩 When the rule breaks

One ROIC snapshot can mislead: a young unit may still be building; a mature one may run on fully depreciated assets. Riskier units need higher hurdles. Closing a unit that supplies another can hurt both.

🏢 In real companies

Family and state-linked conglomerates are common across the Gulf. Head office can move money faster than markets, but often does not, because units are tied to family branches or national priorities. Fix: an annual capital review and ROIC-linked incentives.

💼 In an interview

“How should a group allocate capital across its business units?”

As an internal capital market. Each unit competes for capital on return on invested capital against its risk-adjusted hurdle. I route money to the units earning above the hurdle and pull it from those below — even long-held core businesses — through fix, sell or harvest plans. Autonomy for unit leaders is fine, but it always answers to the cost of capital.

📖 Key terms
Internal capital market
Head office deciding which units get capital, instead of outside investors.
Cross-subsidy
Using strong units’ cash to keep weak units funded.
Risk-adjusted hurdle
A unit’s own minimum return: higher for riskier businesses.
