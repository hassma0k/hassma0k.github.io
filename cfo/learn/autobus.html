Starve the Losers

Internal capital markets · ROIC-based allocation

6 min read · 3-question self-check · practise it in the app

Several business units want capital: which earn their keep, which waste it, and how hard should you starve the losers?

⚡In 30 seconds

  • A unit earning less than its capital costs destroys value, however profitable.
  • Route capital to the units earning most above their hurdle.
  • Equal shares feel fair and quietly subsidise losers with winners’ money.
  • Capital does not care how long you have owned a business.

🎯What you’ll learn

  • Judge each unit by return on capital against its hurdle, not by profit.
  • Move a group’s capital from below-hurdle units to above-hurdle ones.
  • Explain why equal shares and loyalty to “core” units destroy value.

💡The idea

Picture a household with one salary pot. One family member borrows at 8.4% to earn 3.5%, works hard, and proudly reports a profit. The household is still poorer every year. A business unit earning 3.5% on SAR 150M reports SAR 5.25M profit, but its capital costs SAR 12.6M: it destroys SAR 7.35M a year.

A group is an internal capital market: head office decides who gets money, like investors choosing companies. The scorecard is ROIC (return on invested capital): after-tax operating profit ÷ capital tied up. Route capital to units with the widest spread above their hurdle, and pull it from those below. Equal shares feel fair but make winners subsidise losers.

🧮The maths

Return on invested capital

ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Invested capital

NOPAT
operating profit after tax, before financing costs
Invested capital
fixed assets plus working capital tied up in the unit

Unit EVA

EVA = (ROIC − Hurdle) × Invested capital

ROIC
the unit’s return on invested capital
Hurdle
group WACC, adjusted for the unit’s own risk
Invested capital
capital the unit holds or requests

✍️Worked example: Six units, one capital round

WACC 8.4%, SAR 420M to allocate, 7 capacity points. Digital bank: 120M, 16% ROIC, effort 4. Logistics: 100M, 13%, 3. Retail: 90M, 11%, 3. Healthcare: 140M, 9.5%, 4. Real estate: 130M, 6%, 2. Legacy telecom: 150M, 3.5%, 3.

The working, step by step
  1. EVA = capital × (ROIC − 8.4%): Digital bank 120 × 7.6% = 9.12; Logistics 4.60; Retail 2.34; Healthcare 1.54.
  2. Below the line: Real estate 130 × (−2.4%) = −3.12; Telecom 150 × (−4.9%) = −7.35.
  3. The four need 14 points; only 7 exist. Best pair: Digital bank + Logistics, SAR 220M, EVA 9.12 + 4.60 = 13.72.
  4. The “loyal” slate: Telecom “because it is core” + Logistics, 6 points: EVA −7.35 + 4.60 = −2.75 a year.
  5. Leftover 420 − 220 = 200. Idle at 2%: 200 × (2% − 8.4%) = −12.80, leaving just 0.92. Returned: keep 13.72.

Answer. Back the digital bank and logistics arm, refuse the telecom and real estate, and return SAR 200M: SAR 13.72M of EVA a year.

🎮How it plays in the game

  • Runs on the City engine: each card is a unit’s request, showing its return (read as ROIC), cost, payback and effort dots.

  • Capacity (9, 7 or 6 points) usually lets you back two or three units. The Board grades you against the best allocation that fits.

⚠️Common mistakes

✗ Judging a unit by profit, not return on capital.

✓ Put capital next to profit: SAR 5.25M profit on SAR 150M at 8.4% loses SAR 7.35M.

✗ Giving every unit an equal share to keep the peace.

✓ Allocate by spread above each unit’s hurdle, and publish the rule.

✗ Protecting a long-held “core” unit by default.

✓ Set a time-bound plan to fix, harvest or exit it, with a target ROIC.

✅Self-check

Three questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1The legacy telecom holds SAR 150M and earns a 3.5% ROIC. Its head says: “We made SAR 5.25M profit, so we deserve our capital.” The hurdle is 8.4%. What is its EVA?

EVA = (3.5% − 8.4%) × 150 = −7.35M. The profit is real, but the capital charge is 150 × 8.4% = 12.6M. −12.6M forgets the unit earns something.

2The CEO proposes an equal share of capital for each of six units “so everyone stays motivated”. What is the strongest objection?

A healthy average can hide units that destroy value. Equal shares starve the best units and feed the losers. Autonomy over operations is not autonomy over the cost of capital.

3The healthcare venture asks for SAR 140M, promising 9.5%. Group WACC is 8.4%, but its higher risk gives it a 10.5% risk-adjusted hurdle. What should you conclude?

Use the hurdle that matches the risk: 140 × (9.5% − 10.5%) = −1.4M. The +1.54M uses the group WACC. Shareholders can diversify for themselves.

🔎Go deeper

⚖️ The trade-off

Starving a unit has costs: managers may leave, customers may notice, and the unit can lose the scale it needs. Exits bring sale discounts and separation costs. But every riyal left earning 3.5% could earn 16% elsewhere or go back to owners.

🧩 When the rule breaks

One ROIC snapshot can mislead: a young unit may still be building, a mature one may run on old, fully depreciated assets. Riskier units need higher hurdles. Closing a unit that supplies another can hurt both. Test every “core” claim in riyals.

🏢 In real companies

Family and state-linked conglomerates are common across the Gulf. Head office can move money faster than outside markets, but often does not, because units are tied to family branches or national priorities. Investors may apply a conglomerate discount. Useful tools: an annual capital review where every unit bids against its hurdle, and ROIC-linked incentives.

💼 In an interview

“How should a group allocate capital across its business units?”

As an internal capital market. Each unit competes for capital on return on invested capital against its risk-adjusted hurdle. I route money to the units earning above the hurdle and pull it from those below — even long-held core businesses — through fix, sell or harvest plans. Autonomy for unit leaders is fine, but it always answers to the cost of capital.

📖 Key terms
Internal capital market
Head office deciding which units get capital, instead of outside investors.
ROIC
After-tax operating profit divided by the capital tied up in the unit.
Cross-subsidy
Using strong units’ cash to keep weak units funded.
Risk-adjusted hurdle
A unit-specific minimum return: higher for riskier businesses, lower for steadier ones.
Conglomerate discount
Investors valuing a group below what its units would be worth separately.
